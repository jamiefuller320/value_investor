LIQUIDITY AND CAPITAL RESOURCES The primary objectives of Seaboard’s financing strategy are to effectively manage financial risks, ensure efficient liquidity for daily global operations and maintain balance sheet strength. Seaboard’s principal funding sources are generated from operating activities, investments and borrowings from revolving lines of credit and term loans. Seaboard’s cash requirements primarily include funding for working capital, capital expenditures, strategic investments and other needs. Seaboard evaluates its overall liquidity at least on a quarterly basis, and management believes Seaboard’s combination of cash, liquidity and borrowing capabilities will be adequate to meet all and commitments. As of June 28, 2025, Seaboard had total net working capital of $2.1 billion, which includes $1.1 billion of cash and investments. Of the total cash and investments balance, $114 million was held by foreign subsidiaries. Seaboard considers substantially all foreign profits permanently reinvested in its foreign operations, except for undistributed earnings of Seaboard Marine. For all other foreign subsidiaries, Seaboard intends to continue permanently reinvesting their funds outside the U.S. as they continue to demonstrate no need to repatriate them to fund Seaboard’s U.S. operations for the foreseeable future. Seaboard has not recorded deferred taxes for state or foreign withholding taxes that would result upon repatriation of these funds to the U.S. as determination of the tax is not practical due to the complexity of the tax environment in which Seaboard operates. The effective tax rate for the three- and periods of 2025 increased compared to the same periods of 2024 primarily as a result of a decrease in the amount of investment tax credits generated and the impact of incremental Pillar Two taxes. The Organization for Economic Cooperation released Pillar Two as part of its Base Erosion and Profit Shifting initiative to ensure large multinational companies pay a minimum level of tax on the income arising in each jurisdiction where they operate. The adoption and effective dates of these rules vary by country and the rules include some temporary safe harbors. Several countries in which Seaboard operates enacted Pillar Two laws in 2024, and additional countries in which Seaboard has material operations, including the Isle of Man and The Bahamas, adopted Pillar Two rules effective in 2025. These jurisdictions had effective tax rates that were lower than 15% prior to implementing the new rules. Seaboard will continue to monitor legislative developments related to Pillar Two in the countries in which it operates and the potential impact on future results. On July 4, 2025, the U.S. President signed into law the OBBBA. Seaboard is currently evaluating the potential impact of the OBBBA on its deferred tax balances and other changes required to its financial statements, which will be reflected in the third quarter 10-Q as the enactment date was after period end. Operational Risks (1) Changes in and Uncertainty of U.S. Trade Policy and the Impact of Any Tariffs, Trade Sanctions or Similar Government Actions Could Adversely Impact Seaboard’s Business . The U.S. recently instituted certain changes, and proposed additional changes, in trade policies that include the negotiation or termination of trade agreements, the imposition of higher tariffs on imports into the U.S., and other government regulations affecting trade between the U.S. and other countries where Seaboard conducts business. Global trade disruption, significant introductions of trade barriers and bilateral trade frictions, or countermeasures imposed in response to such government actions, have and could further i) reduce Seaboard’s ability to sell products in certain geographical markets or globally, ii) increase the costs for Seaboard’s imported materials and equipment, or iii) lower overall revenues and margins, any of which could have a material adverse impact on Seaboard’s business and financial condition. Specifically, certain previously exported products for Seaboard Foods may have to be rendered in the U.S. if other markets cannot be identified, which would negatively impact margins further. Changes in tariffs and trade restrictions can be, and have been, announced with little or no advance notice, and are difficult to predict, which makes the associated risks difficult to anticipate and mitigate, if even possible. If Seaboard is unable to navigate further changes in U.S. or international trade policy, it could have a material adverse impact on Seaboard’s business and financial condition.
