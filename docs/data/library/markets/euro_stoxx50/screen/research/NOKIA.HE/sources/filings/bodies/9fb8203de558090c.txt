into Ericsson and Nokia Combined headcount for Vodafone and Three appears to have fallen 12% since before the merger, Vodafone's just-published annual report shows. Iain Morris , International Editor , Light Reading May 28, 2026 6 Min Read VodafoneThree CEO Max Taylor (pictured) is presiding over job cuts at the UK mobile operator. (Source: Iain Morris/Light Reading) The architects of AI have been quickly retracting their rhetoric about the technology's threat to jobs, after student uproar showed that the prospective next-generation workforce would not settle for economic obsolescence without a fight. But telecom's big operators haven't needed AI to cut hundreds of thousands of jobs in the last decade. Last year's merger between Vodafone and Three looked like further glum news for the UK's telecom-sector workforce as the companies targeted cost savings of £700 million (US$940 million). The annual report Vodafone has just published seems to illustrate the damage so far. Before the merger was finalized exactly 12 months ago, Vodafone employed an average of 9,332 people at its UK segment for the fiscal year ending in March 2025. Three still had 4,800 employees on its books in December 2024, several months before the deal went through, according to a Three spokesperson at the time. Combined, this would obviously have given the newly created VodafoneThree a total headcount of 14,132 employees if nothing changed. Yet Vodafone's annual report puts the average size of the UK workforce at 12,416 employees for the recently ended fiscal year. The difference of 1,716 equals about 12% of the previous total or roughly one in eight jobs. The reduction so far could have been even more aggressive , of course, and no one would have expected merging private-sector organizations to retain duplicate roles out of charitable concern for people's welfare. What's potentially more interesting is whether the government conditions attached to the merger are forcing VodafoneThree to cut more deeply than it originally planned and retreat from some of its former principles. Epic downsizing It should be noted, first, that the two operators had already undergone some epic downsizing before they decided to pair up. Back in 2017/18, Vodafone's UK segment employed as many as 12,379 people and the number had fallen from 13,238 the year before. In 2019, Three put its headcount at 5,400. Across the combined total of the two operators, about 30% of roles appear to have gone since then. Jobs are known to have been cut by Three as part of an IT rationalization under previous management. As for Vodafone, some of the headcount reduction at the UK segment can probably be explained by the movement of staff from country-specific departments into "common functions" serving multiple geographical markets. Indeed, the number of staff employed in common functions by Vodafone soared from 24,441 in 2018 to 37,550 in the most recent fiscal year. Overall group headcount fell from 98,103 in 2022 to 92,988 last year as Vodafone withdrew from several markets. Under the merger agreement reached with the UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), VodafoneThree is required to meet a series of undisclosed targets for the activation of spectrum at its mobile sites to support 4G and 5G services. To ensure it does not fall behind schedule and risk penalties, it is leaning much more heavily on the managed services expertise of Ericsson and Nokia, its two radio access network (RAN) vendors, than Vodafone and Three would previously have done. Iain Milligan, VodafoneThree's director of network development and infrastructure, explained the thinking behind that last month. Ericsson and Nokia are playing a key part in site solution design and have assumed responsibility for some of the work formerly carried out by telco staff, he told Light Reading at the FutureNet World event in London. The operator was driven to use those vendors by the need to meet its CMA-imposed obligations. It did not have the luxury of testing alternatives and being "the ones to pull it all together," said Milligan. Ericsson and Nokia, in any case, might have seemed the obvious choices in a shrinking RAN market that today supports very few vendors, with Huawei and ZTE banned from selling products to UK telcos. But they were not Vodafone's obvious choices before it acquired Three (having originally owned 51% of the merged company, Vodafone took full control this month). Supposedly a big fan of the "open RAN" concept, Vodafone was previously eyeing a Samsung-led deployment of the technology across about 2,500 sites. Representing about 14% of Vodafone's entire pre-merger RAN footprint, those sites have historically been equipped by Huawei, which UK operators are required to remove from their networks by the end of next year under government orders. Yet building a true open RAN would have meant joining Samsung's RAN products to radios, IT hardware and cloud-computing systems supplied by other companies. Cognizant of the effort that would be involved, and keen to be more self-reliant, Vodafone expected to handle much of the necessary systems integration. In late 2021, Vodafone Group had announced plans to add about 7,000 software engineers to a team of 9,000 at the time. "The commercial benefit comes from reducing our dependence on systems integrators and spending less with them and developing more of our own capabilities and open architecture," said Scott Petty , Vodafone Group's chief technology officer, during a press conference back then. "Our headcount will grow and our external workforce will reduce." So long, Samsung Vodafone, however, made almost no progress on switching from Huawei to Samsung while it waited for UK authorities to approve or disallow its merger with Three. Around the time the deal was finally blessed, it had evidently decided it would not be able to hit the government-mandated deployment targets efficiently with the South Korean vendor. The upshot is that VodafoneThree, for better or worse, today looks neither very self-reliant nor at all invested in open RAN. The post-merger priority was obtaining "a commitment for ease of delivery and pace of delivery, and that's where Nokia and Ericsson showed they could give us the pace and volumes we need," said Milligan. Opportunities for any job cuts, though, are unlikely to be confined to this RAN area. On stage at FutureNet, Andrea Dona, VodafoneThree's chief network officer, said he has also been working to shut down "legacy" systems at the business. "Yes, we have removed entire departments in VodafoneThree," he told the audience. Outside his technology remit, there will have been obvious overlap between Vodafone and Three in areas such as accounts, human resources and the like. A spokesperson said cuts have focused mainly on the elimination of duplicate "head office" roles while insisting there are no planned layoffs on the retail side of the business. The merger could invigorate 5G connectivity in the UK as VodafoneThree's obligations maintain pressure on rival telcos BT and Virgin Media O2. This, rather than open RAN, was the government's priority. For Vodafone and Three, it was the view that serving all their customers on a single network – even one with a bigger number of sites – would ultimately turn out to be much more profitable. Unfortunately, for people who work in the UK's telecom industry, the workforce shrinkage goes on.
