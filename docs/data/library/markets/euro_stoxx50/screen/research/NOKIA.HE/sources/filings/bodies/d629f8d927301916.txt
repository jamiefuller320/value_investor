HECTOR RETAMAL/AFP via Getty Images Nokia is shutting down virtually every remaining operation it has in mainland China — not just the Hangzhou radio technology lab that drew attention last week, but all sites across Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Qingdao, and beyond — completing an exit that will erase a presence the Finnish vendor built from a single joint venture in 1984 into one of the most significant foreign commercial footprints in the country's telecommunications industry. The South China Morning Post reported Nokia's full mainland exit on Tuesday; a Nokia spokesperson confirmed the decision via email to Reuters. Nokia's ADR shares fell nearly 4% in premarket trading — to approximately $10.38 — in early Tuesday trading. Greater China revenues that once exceeded €2.2 billion (approximately $2.55 billion) annually had already fallen below €913 million (approximately $1.06 billion) by 2025, a collapse of more than 58% in seven years. The full mainland closure will eliminate the majority of Nokia's approximately 7,200-person Greater China workforce by December 31, 2026. Nokia employed roughly 7,200 people across mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan at the end of 2025, with sites in Beijing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Chengdu, and Qingdao, according to Nokia's annual report . The staged layoffs are planned in batches through December 31, 2026, affecting both Nokia's mobile networks and network infrastructure divisions. Only after-sales support functions are expected to remain once the phased cuts are complete. Nokia Told Staff in August: This Is a Full Exit Nokia communicated the broader shutdown to employees via internal video conference from its Helsinki headquarters earlier in August — the same process by which the Hangzhou closure reached employees on August 13. The Hangzhou facility had been Nokia's most substantial China research hub, developing radio technology at the core of its AirScale 5G base stations — the antenna systems through which wireless devices connect to 5G networks. Its roughly 1,600 positions were the first to go; what the South China Morning Post confirmed on Tuesday is that the Hangzhou closure was not a standalone event. The South China Morning Post confirmed that all Nokia mainland sites will close by year-end — not just Hangzhou. One Shanghai-based source described the plan as "basically a gradual exit from the market." Nokia's spokesperson declined to provide specific headcount figures but confirmed the company is "adjusting our operational footprint in China to address this reality" after sustained business decline . Only after-sales support functions are expected to remain once the phased cuts — structured in batches through December 31 — are complete. Read more: Nokia Closes China R&D Hub as 5G World Splits Into Rival Supply Chains Revenue Fell 58% Over Seven Years — Then Came the Geopolitics The numbers behind Nokia's decision are unambiguous. In 2018, Nokia's Greater China region generated nearly €2.2 billion (approximately $2.55 billion) in annual revenues, making it one of Nokia's most valuable single-country markets globally. By 2025, that figure had dropped to €913 million (approximately $1.06 billion) — a 58% decline in seven years, including a 19% drop in 2025 alone. Nokia's radio access network market share in China fell below 3% combined with Swedish rival Ericsson, prompting Dell'Oro Group vice president and RAN analyst Stefan Pongratz to describe the two vendors' combined China footprint as "negligible." The business collapse was not purely a product-competitiveness failure. By September 2025, Nokia executives had disclosed to journalists in Oulu, Finland, that the company had received signals it was being excluded from China on national-security grounds — the exact mirror image of how Western governments have treated Huawei and ZTE. Tommi Uitto, then Nokia's mobile networks president, stated it directly at the Oulu press conference: Nokia and Ericsson were being excluded from China for national security reasons, with their combined China RAN share having fallen to 3%. Ericsson's trajectory follows the same arc with striking precision. Its China revenues surged to approximately SEK 18.7 billion (approximately $1.96 billion) in 2020 after it won 5G contracts with China Mobile — then collapsed after Sweden banned Huawei from its own 5G networks in October 2020. By 2025, Ericsson's China revenues had fallen to between SEK 5.9 billion and SEK 8.1 billion (approximately $620 million to $851 million), or roughly 3% of its global total. Asymmetry: Huawei Holds 59% of Germany's 5G Sites While Nokia Holds Under 3% in China Nokia CEO Justin Hotard has named the asymmetry with precision. "Why do we allow high-risk vendors in Europe in our networks, particularly when they don't allow us to play in their markets?" he asked publicly, noting that Nokia and Ericsson together hold less than 3% of China's radio access network market while Huawei alone accounts for approximately 59% of Germany's installed 5G base stations, according to Strand Consult data cited by Germany's interior ministry. That imbalance reflects a policy gap with a closing deadline: Germany's government has mandated that operators reduce Chinese vendor components in their 5G radio access networks to a maximum of 25% by October 1, 2026 — a cutoff now fewer than six weeks away. The European Union's 5G security toolbox, which recommends restricting high-risk vendors, had been implemented by only 13 of 27 member states as of 2025. John Strand, the founder of Danish advisory firm Strand Consult, offered a starker assessment in January 2026: "They will find that the deadline for submitting a bid to operators in China is shorter than the time it takes to be approved by the authorities to bid for projects. China is lost land for vendors like Ericsson and Nokia. The strategic loss is specific in a way that the numbers alone do not capture. Nokia's 5G base stations — the AirScale systems that Hangzhou's engineers helped develop — use massive MIMO (arrays of 64 to 128 antenna elements) with beamforming technology to focus directional radio signals toward individual user devices, increasing network capacity and spectral efficiency. Nokia's next-generation platform, built on Nvidia's Aerial AI-RAN software and CUDA-powered GPUs, takes this further: instead of rule-based signal processing, it uses machine learning models to optimize beamforming and interference management in real time. Those machine learning models require training data — real-world radio signal patterns from base stations operating at scale, in dense urban environments, across a full range of interference conditions. China is where that data exists at the necessary scale. China operates approximately 4.83 million 5G base stations across 330 cities, deploying 5G-Advanced technologies at a pace that outpaces Western carriers by 12 to 18 months. The radio signal data generated by that deployment environment — dense urban interference patterns, beamforming performance at extreme cell density — is the training input that 6G AI-RAN systems need to become competitive. Nokia's zero-presence status in mainland China closes that data channel before the 3GPP standards body finalizes 6G's core architecture in its December 2028 protocol freeze — the deadline at which the technology choices embedded in the standard become permanent. When Nokia still had any operational presence in China, even at under-3% market share, it retained some access to the world's most active 5G deployment environment. With zero presence, that access ends — and it ends before the window during which the access would matter most. Nokia launched its commercial AI-RAN platform on July 15, 2026, with Nvidia and is targeting T-Mobile for field trials before the end of 2026, with commercial service expected in 2027. The platform is technically competitive. The data foundation it is being trained on is not. What Nokia Gets From Exiting: €200 Million in Annual Savings, AI Growth, and a Cleaner Balance Sheet Nokia's December 2025 acquisition of full ownership of Nokia Shanghai Bell — the joint venture it had shared with state-backed China Huaxin since 2017 — for CNY 4.1 billion (approximately $609 million at current exchange rates) gave Nokia the unilateral authority to make these cuts without navigating joint-venture politics. Nokia's 2026 restructuring budget was raised from €250 million (approximately $290 million) to €800 million (approximately $927 million), with approximately €350 million (approximately $406 million) allocated to China specifically — covering severance, lease terminations, and the completion of the Nokia Shanghai Bell integration. Nokia expects approximately €200 million (approximately $232 million) in annualized cost savings from the China restructuring. That savings estimate competes with a much larger growth story. Nokia reported that AI and cloud order intake reached €2.8 billion (approximately $3.24 billion) in the second quarter of 2026, a 105% increase over the prior-year quarter. CEO Justin Hotard also announced an additional €200 million (approximately $232 million) in European restructuring charges — a broader cost-reduction program that analysts estimated could affect more than 2,000 European positions. Nokia's stock had reached a multi-year closing high of $16.77 on June 2, 2026, driven by investor enthusiasm for its AI infrastructure business before Tuesday's China exit news interrupted that trajectory. Nokia's Q2 2026 comparable operating profit guidance: €2.1 billion to €2.6 billion (approximately $2.43 billion to $3.01 billion) for the full year. End of a 42-Year Relationship That Shaped an Era Nokia's China roots trace to 1984, when Shanghai Bell was established as a joint venture between China Post and Telecommunications and Belgium's Bell Telephone Manufacturing — one of the first Sino-foreign telecom ventures the country had ever seen. Belgium's Bell was later absorbed by France's Alcatel, which Nokia then acquired in 2016 in its €15.6 billion Alcatel-Lucent purchase . Nokia Shanghai Bell, the renamed successor to the original joint venture, became Nokia's operational vehicle in China from 2017. At its peak, Nokia and its predecessor companies employed approximately 13,700 people across Greater China. That figure had fallen to approximately 7,200 by the end of 2025. By December 31, 2026, the majority of those remaining positions will be gone. Analysts from the Center for European Policy Analysis, writing in August 2026, concluded that China won the 5G competition through a combination of state financing, technology investment, and aggressive domestic deployment — and that the US and Europe will prevail in the 6G contest only if they can field a more technologically advanced and economically competitive alternative. In March 2026, the Global Coalition on Telecoms — bringing together the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, Australia, Sweden, and Finland — announced its intention to embed security requirements into 6G specifications before the December 2028 protocol freeze, explicitly drawing on what went wrong in the 5G era. Nokia's total China exit is, in that framing, not a business setback to be managed. It is the closing of the last chapter of a 40-year commercial relationship — and the opening of a 6G competition in which Nokia will build its AI-native radio technology without access to the planet's largest existing field laboratory. Frequently Asked Questions Why is Nokia closing all its China operations in 2026? Nokia's mainland China business has declined by more than 58% in revenues since 2018, falling from approximately €2.2 billion (approximately $2.55 billion) to €913 million (approximately $1.06 billion) in 2025. Nokia executives disclosed in September 2025 that the company had received signals of exclusion from China on national-security grounds — the same mechanism Western governments have applied to Huawei and ZTE. With market share below 3% and no viable path back to meaningful Chinese contracts, Nokia decided to wind down all mainland operations by December 31, 2026, according to Nokia's business decline reporting . How does Nokia's total China exit differ from what was reported last week? The prior reporting (including TechTimes on August 15) covered Nokia's confirmed closure of its Hangzhou radio technology R&D facility, which affects approximately 1,600 employees. The SCMP report published August 18 and confirmed by Nokia on Tuesday reveals that the full scope of the exit extends to all Nokia mobile networks and network infrastructure sites in mainland China — including Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, and Qingdao — with staged layoffs targeting completion by December 31, 2026. Only after-sales functions are expected to survive. The scale is qualitatively different: not one hub, but a near-total exit, as SCMP first reported . Why does Nokia's China exit matter for 6G — which is still years away? Nokia's AI-RAN platform — built with Nvidia's GPU technology — requires real-world training data from large-scale 5G deployments to optimize AI-based beamforming and interference management. China's 4.83 million base stations across 330 cities represent by far the world's largest 5G deployment environment, operating 12 to 18 months ahead of Western carriers in advanced 5G-A technology. Nokia's zero-presence status in China closes that data channel before the 3GPP standards body finalizes 6G's core architecture in its December 2028 protocol freeze. Once that freeze happens, the architectural choices are locked in. Nokia is building 6G AI-RAN technology from a narrower data foundation than its Chinese competitors will have, as detailed in Nokia and Nvidia's AI-RAN launch . What happens to Nokia employees in China? Nokia has not publicly disclosed the exact number of positions to be eliminated, but has approximately 7,200 employees across mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan as of end-2025. The majority of mainland China positions face elimination in phased batches through December 31, 2026. At least one former Nokia software testing employee in Shanghai reported receiving an "N+3" severance package — three months of additional pay beyond statutory requirements — though Nokia has not confirmed this as a company-wide standard.
