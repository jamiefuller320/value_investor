Key Elements Nokia Huawei ZTE China Mobile China Telecom China Unicom Ericsson Nvidia Justin Hotard Tommi Uitto Finnish telecom equipment giant Nokia is reportedly planning to lay off most of its mainland China workforce by the end of 2026 and phase out nearly all of its local operating sites. After more than 40 years of deep engagement in the Chinese market, Nokia may retain only an after-sales service team, effectively marking a gradual exit from what was once its largest single-country market globally. Market sources indicate that Nokia held an internal video conference earlier this month from its Helsinki headquarters, announcing to its China team the plan to scale back local operations. Both the China mobile networks and network infrastructure divisions will undergo phased layoffs, with most workforce adjustments expected to be completed by the end of this year, though the specific number of affected employees remains unclear. According to Nokia's annual report, as of the end of 2025, the company had approximately 7,200 employees across China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan, with sites in cities including Beijing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Chengdu, and Qingdao. A Shanghai-based source familiar with the matter revealed that after the restructuring is complete, Nokia may retain only after-sales service in China, essentially amounting to a gradual exit from the local market. A former employee who previously worked in Nokia's Shanghai software testing division said he left the company in recent weeks and received an "N+3" severance package—compensation calculated based on years of service, plus an additional three months' salary. A Nokia spokesperson did not confirm the specific number of layoffs or the list of sites to be closed, but said the company had previously announced measures to align its China operations more closely with its global business. As the China business has continued to shrink in recent years, the company is adjusting its local operating footprint to reflect current market conditions. Nokia declined to confirm whether it plans to gradually close all of its operating locations in China by year-end, but did confirm the closure of its Hangzhou site. The South China Morning Post (SCMP) later reported exclusively that the downsizing extends beyond the Hangzhou site to a comprehensive withdrawal covering Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, and Qingdao, with a Nokia spokesperson confirming the decision via email to Reuters. The Rise and Fall in the Chinese Market Nokia was once the dominant player in the global mobile phone market. In the early 2000s, the Chinese market was critically important to Nokia, at one point serving as its largest single-country market worldwide. However, with the advent of the smartphone era, Nokia steadily lost ground in consumer electronics, ultimately selling its handset business to Microsoft and pivoting to focus on telecom network infrastructure equipment. In the telecom equipment space, Nokia and Ericsson have long ranked among the world's top three suppliers, but in recent years they have faced intense competition in the Chinese market from domestic players such as Huawei and ZTE. China's three major telecom operators—China Mobile, China Telecom, and China Unicom—adopted domestic equipment extensively during the peak of 5G network construction, causing foreign suppliers' market share to shrink steadily. Nokia and its predecessor companies' headcount in Greater China has also declined from a peak of approximately 13,700 to roughly 7,200 by the end of 2025. Revenue data cited by industry publication Light Reading shows Nokia's Greater China annual revenue has shrunk from approximately €2.2 billion (about $2.55 billion) in 2018 to €913 million (about $1.06 billion) in 2025—a decline of over 58% over seven years, including a 19% drop in 2025 alone. Stefan Pongratz, Vice President and RAN (Radio Access Network) analyst at market research firm Dell'Oro Group, noted that Nokia and Ericsson's combined share of China's RAN market has fallen below 3%, describing their current presence in the market as "negligible." Ericsson's trajectory has been nearly identical: its China revenue surged to approximately SEK 18.7 billion (about $1.96 billion) in 2020 after winning a 5G contract from China Mobile, then plummeted following Sweden's October 2020 ban on Huawei 5G equipment, falling to just SEK 5.9 billion to SEK 8.1 billion (about $620 million to $850 million) in 2025, representing roughly 3% of its global revenue. Geopolitical Factors: From Commercial Competition to National Security Exclusion Nokia's decline in the Chinese market is not simply a matter of losing out to domestic competitors' technological catch-up. Tommi Uitto, President of Nokia's Mobile Networks division, told media in Oulu, Finland in September 2025 that the company had received indications that Nokia is being excluded from the Chinese market on national security grounds—mirroring the approach Western countries have taken toward Huawei and ZTE. Nokia CEO Justin Hotard raised pointed questions about reciprocity: "Why do we allow high-risk vendors into European networks, especially when they don't allow us into their markets?" He noted that Nokia and Ericsson combined hold less than 3% of China's RAN market, while Huawei accounts for approximately 59% of installed 5G base stations in Germany. The German government has required telecom operators to reduce the share of Chinese supplier components to below 25% by October 1, 2026—a deadline now less than six weeks away. John Strand, founder of Danish consultancy Strand Consult, stated bluntly in January 2026: "They will find that the deadline for submitting bids to Chinese operators is shorter than the time needed to obtain regulatory approval to bid. For vendors like Ericsson and Nokia, China is lost territory." The Hidden Cost for 6G: AI-RAN Needs China's Training Data Nokia has in recent years bet its next-generation base station technology on the AI-RAN platform developed in partnership with Nvidia—using machine learning models to optimize signal beamforming and interference management in real time, replacing traditional rules-based signal processing. The platform was officially unveiled with Nvidia on July 15, 2026, and is slated for field testing with U.S. carrier T-Mobile, targeting commercial deployment in 2027. The challenge is that training such machine learning models requires large-scale, high-density real-world wireless signal data from urban environments—and China, with approximately 4.83 million 5G base stations covering 330 cities, represents the world's highest-density deployment environment, with technology deployment running 12 to 18 months ahead of Western telecom operators. Nokia's complete exit from the Chinese market means it will lose access to this largest source of real-world testing data before the 3GPP standards body freezes the 6G core architecture in December 2028. A study by the Center for European Policy Analysis (CEPA) in August 2026 concluded that China has already won the 5G race through state funding, technology investment, and aggressive deployment, and that the U.S. and Europe can only win the 6G race by offering more competitive technological and economic alternatives. The Global Context of Strategic Contraction Nokia has been steadily adjusting its global operating footprint in recent years, concentrating resources on high-growth markets including North America, Europe, and India. In the United States, Nokia has benefited from 5G infrastructure subsidies and the Open RAN trend, securing numerous orders from telecom operators. In India, Nokia and Ericsson jointly dominate the country's 5G network deployment. By contrast, the Chinese market's contribution to Nokia's revenue has shrunk dramatically, and amid rising geopolitical tensions, the long-term prospects for foreign equipment vendors in China are fraught with uncertainty. If the layoff and site closure plans are fully implemented, this would represent Nokia's most significant strategic contraction from the Chinese market in decades. Notably, Nokia's R&D sites in China have historically shouldered some global product development responsibilities. After these sites are closed, those functions are expected to be transferred to R&D centers in India, Southeast Asia, or Europe. In December 2025, Nokia acquired full ownership of the Shanghai Bell joint venture for RMB 4.1 billion (approximately $609 million)—the company was previously jointly held by Nokia and China Huaxin. With full control secured, Nokia gained the unilateral authority to decide on layoffs and site closures without needing to negotiate with its joint venture partner. The company's 2026 restructuring budget has been raised from €250 million to €800 million (approximately $927 million), of which roughly €350 million (approximately $406 million) is earmarked specifically for China, covering severance costs, lease terminations, and Shanghai Bell integration work, with estimated annualized cost savings of approximately €200 million (approximately $232 million). In contrast to the contraction of its China business, Nokia's AI and cloud business is showing strong momentum: second-quarter 2026 AI and cloud orders reached €2.8 billion (approximately $3.24 billion), up 105% year-over-year. Industry analysts note that while Nokia's move may incur one-time costs such as severance payments in the short term, it will help reduce operating expenses over the long term and allow the company to focus resources on markets with greater growth potential. Amid intensifying competition in the global telecom equipment market, Nokia's strategic pivot reflects the structural challenges foreign technology companies face in the Chinese market.
