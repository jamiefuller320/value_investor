Flowsheet Mining The Tiris ore is hosted in shallow, flat-lying calcrete. Mineralisation starts about 10 centimetres below surface. The ore zone is typically 5 to 15 metres thick. It is free-digging throughout, so no drilling or blasting is required. The mine plan delivers 111.1 million tonnes of ore over 26 years at an average grade of 225 ppm U ₃ O ₈ . About 111.4 million tonnes of waste will also be moved, giving a strip ratio of 1.00:1. Aura will run the mine itself, using excavators and trucks across several shallow pits. Waste and tailings go back into mined-out pits, and Aura will rehabilitate the ground as mining moves on. No permanent waste dumps or tailings dams are needed. The pits designed for the project contain 11.7 million pounds of U ₃ O ₈ more than the mine plan extracts. All of this extra material is Inferred Mineral Resource. It sits at Marie, Hippolyte West, Hippolyte East and the southern end of Hippolyte South. Adding it would have pushed the share of Inferred material in the final years of the plan above 50%. The mine plan therefore leaves it out. Aura expects to test this material in future resource work. Aura is also investigating methods to make the mining fleet more efficient and safer. The BFS mine plan adopts a fleet of autonomous, battery-electric haul trucks, peaking at 23 trucks, managed remotely using an autonomous haulage system (AHS). These will run around the clock and can be charged from the site’s solar power, cutting diesel use. Future work will focus on integrating sensors into excavators, haul trucks, belts, and transfer stations, enabling ore to be scanned for geochemical and mineralogical properties prior to processing. This information would reach the plant before the ore does, allowing the Processing Plant to adjust its settings in advance to suit each load. Given the investment in the communication infrastructure with AHS, tele-remote operations for excavator, loader, and dozer will also be investigated. Figure 1: Tiris Process Flowsheet Processing First, the ore is washed and tumbled in a scrubber to break it up. It is then passed through three screens: 2 millimetres, 300 microns and, finally, 75 microns. The uranium is in the fine particles that pass through the 75-micron screen. The coarse rock left on the screen holds very little uranium, and is returned to the pit void. This step removes about 88% of ore by weight over the life-of-mine (LOM). The beneficiation process recovers about 91.9% of the uranium and raises the grade from 225 ppm to 1,697 ppm U 3 O 8 , a seven-fold increase (life of mine average). Rejecting most of the ore mass at this stage keeps the leach plant small and operating costs low. This process occurs at one of two Beneficiation Plants, each with a design capacity of 2.5Mtpa, located near active mining sites. The uranium-concentrated slurry is pumped to the Processing Plant as leach feed. The plant is designed to process 550,000 tonnes of leach feed per year, or about 1,500 tonnes per day, and operates at this rate in most years. Over the 26-year mine life, it averages about 520,000 tonnes per year, mainly driven by the required relocation of the beneficiation plants, which occurs seven times as mining advances into new areas. Each move, along with each shift of the mining face, briefly reduces the ore sent to the plant. Pre-leach dewatering of the beneficiated leach feed is by two-stage centrifugation, with a wash step incorporated in the first stage. The wash rejects soluble salts and other deleterious species that would otherwise interfere with downstream processing. Centrifugation replaces the pressure filtration adopted in the previous study. The dewatered cake then undergoes alkaline leaching, in which sodium carbonate dissolves uranium in a series of stirred tanks. After leaching, the liquids and solids must be separated. The slurry contains dissolved uranium and very fine clay. Clay acts like a sponge, holding on to water and refusing to let go. With ordinary filters, we lose some of the uranium-bearing liquid or need huge, energy-intensive filter plants. We now use ATA®, owned by Clean TeQ Water (ASX: CNQ), to solve this. ATA ® , short for Activator-Tether-Anchor, is a complete dewatering system that conditions the clay slurry and then filters it. Its name comes from the three stages of the process (Activator-Tether-Anchor). In activation, a polymer is mixed into the leached clay slurry, where it coats and agglomerates the fine clay particles. In tethering, a second polymer is mixed into a stream of coarse desert sand, laying down a layer of binding sites across each grain. In anchoring, the two streams are combined, and the activated clay slurry bonds to the tethered sand grains, locking the fines into a granular structure. This granular structure allows the uranium-bearing liquid to drain freely rather than remain trapped within the clay particles. A horizontal vacuum belt filter is then used to separate the liquid from the solids. This open granular structure is the key. Without ATA ® , fine clay packs into a thin, tight layer that blocks the filter cloth and traps the uranium-bearing liquid. Standard chemicals (such as flocculants) can further clump clay particles together, leading to blinding of the filter cloth and entrapment of the uranium-bearing liquid. The granular structure created by ATA® further allows for efficient washing of the cake. Solution is sprayed over the cake in stages to flush out the remaining uranium-bearing liquid. We chose ATA ® for three reasons. First, it works on every type of Tiris ore. It was the only option tested that was shown to do so. Second, it does the job in one step. The previous design needed several. Third, it makes a firm, stackable cake of about 67% solids, rising to 78–79% with vibration-assisted dewatering. This means the leftover solids can go straight back into the empty mine pits. ATA ® was specifically developed to dewater fine mine tailings. Clean TeQ Water has run more than 100 bench-scale filtration tests on Tiris material. The pilot plant at Bir Moghrein, starting in November 2026, will prove ATA ® on Tiris ore before the final investment decision. Next, the uranium-bearing liquid is sent to ion exchange, where it is pumped through columns filled with resin beads. In this process, uranium is adsorbed onto resin beads. This is another key to the flowsheet. We now use Moving Bed Ion Exchange (MBIX), also from Clean TeQ Water. In MBIX, the resin beads are continuously transferred from column to column against the liquid flow, maximising resin utilisation and uranium loading. A second liquid containing sodium bicarbonate then washes the uranium off the beads in a U-shaped column, stripping out impurities and producing a concentrated uranium solution. The uranium is then precipitated (via two stages) into a solid powder, dried, and packed into drums. This product is called yellowcake. The final step treats the process water so that it can be reused and useful reagent chemicals can be recovered. The water is passed through nanofiltration membranes, which allow the water to pass while recovering the chemicals. One set of nanofiltration membranes recovers sodium carbonate from the waste liquid generated by ion exchange, which is then used in the leaching process, and returns it to the start of that process, reducing the amount of fresh sodium carbonate required by approximately 50%. A second set of nanofiltration membranes recovers sodium bicarbonate from the concentrated uranium stream from ion exchange, allowing it to be reused. Finally, reverse osmosis purifies the water so that the plant can reuse it. Aura also plans to digitalise the plant and the mining operations. Enhanced instrumentation will monitor each stage of the process in real time, feeding data into a digital twin (a computer model of the plant that mirrors the behaviour of the physical operation ever more closely as the AI-driven unit process models learn from the plant’s response to changing ore characteristics and operating conditions). Operators can also use the data to test process changes before implementing them in the plant, helping to improve stability, optimise recovery and performance and identify potential issues early. The digital twin is not included in the Study's cost estimate; however, the Company aims to progress this initiative throughout the detailed design and construction phases. Infrastructure The following is a summary of the key infrastructure inputs for the Project: Renewable Power: Power and process steam will be supplied by an independent power producer (IPP) under a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA). The IPP will fund, build, own and operate the generation facility, so power generation carries no development capital for Aura and is included in operating costs. Based on a request-for-proposal process with several large power providers, a power price of US$0.15/kWh and $92/t steam (via diesel boilers) has been used in the study. Prior to execution of a PPA, Aura will work with potential IPPs to produce steam by renewables. Water: The Taoudeni bore field can sustainably supply up to 74 litres per second, with C22 available as a supplementary supply, against a site requirement of 56 litres per second. A bore field and water pipeline from the Taoudeni basin (120km south of the Project) has been included in the BFS capital and operating estimate. Access: Product is trucked about 1,447 km to the Port of Nouakchott, of which 782 km is sealed highway. Rail via the SNIM line has been assessed and remains open for further optimisation. People Every decision we make about the physical assets (mining, processing, infrastructure) takes the nature of human work into consideration. Our objective is to provide Mauritania with the first mining and processing operation of a new age where the jobs on offer provide staff with excellent career development opportunities, intellectual stimulation and quality of life. Safer work: Autonomous mining and plant digitalisation allow the handover of relentlessly repetitive tasks from humans to machines. Exposure to physically and mentally harmful working conditions can be sharply reduced. And because a lean site crew can be supervised from an operations centre in Nouakchott, fewer people need to live away from their families in a remote and often harsh environment. Better careers: Roles can be refocused on problem-solving and decision-making, becoming higher-value-adding, better-paid, and forming the foundation of attractive careers. Over time, such skills improve the quality of individual lives. They will also help strengthen Mauritania's broader economy. Higher productivity: There is no trade-off between the human benefits and business performance. Machines don't get bored, so they perform repetitive tasks with greater predictability. Better jobs attract, motivate and retain better people. And the gains compound: the plant's models learn and never forget with every tonne processed, and one operations centre can oversee several plants at once. Geology and Mineral Resource Regional and local geology The Project lies in the north-eastern, Palaeoproterozoic part of the Reguibat Shield, within the West African Craton. The shield is made up mainly of granites, metasediments and metavolcanics. It is bounded by the Taoudeni Basin to the south, the Tindouf Basin to the north and the Mauritanide fold belt to the west. Two main granite types occur in the resource areas. A grey granite carries about 1–2 ppm uranium. A finer-grained, foliated red granite carries 5–20 ppm uranium and is thought to be the source of the uranium in the deposits. The resource areas lie under very flat ground, covered by desert pavement and thin wind-blown sand. The uranium occurs in weathered red granite and in gravels laid down on or near it. The mineralised gravels and weathered granite sit at the surface or under less than 30 cm of sand. They form thin, flat, continuous sheets 1 to 12 m thick over fresh granite. Mineralisation The uranium occurs as carnotite, a potassium uranium vanadate, possibly with some of the related calcium mineral tyuyamunite. The carnotite is mostly micron-scale. It forms fine dustings and coatings on granite fragments and in patches of calcium carbonate (calcrete). Figure 7: Typical trench showing soft sandy overburden over gravelly, free-digging calcrete ore The deposits formed as saline groundwater leached uranium from the red granites during wetter periods over the past 2.5 million years. The most recent wet period ended about 5,900 years ago. Evaporation in the following dry periods precipitated the uranium vanadates together with carbonates, sulphates and chlorides. Tiris differs from better-known calcrete uranium deposits such as Langer Heinrich in Namibia and Yeelirrie in Western Australia. Those are river valley-fill deposits in clays or hard calcrete below the water table. Tiris formed in shallow depressions in loose gravels and weathered granite, above the water table. This is why the ore is loose and lies at or near the surface, so it can be dug without blasting and needs no crushing. The carnotite forms fine coatings on the gravel and granite fragments, so it separates readily from the host rock. Geometallurgy The main economic drivers of the Tiris flowsheet are the extent of ore upgrading during screening and the management of sulphate that reaches the leach. Geometallurgical domaining predicts how these drivers vary across the resource. The domains are carried into the Mineral Resource block models, the recovery estimates and the mine plan. The 2019 DFS test work used three process-behaviour composites built from a 2018 trench programme at Lazare North and Lazare South. For the BFS, AMC Consultants rebuilt the domaining across the whole resource using the larger assay, mineralogy and screening dataset. The new domaining is now the primary framework for the Project. Geometallurgical groups AMC applied a multivariate statistical method (UMAP) to calcium, aluminium and sulphur assays, which separate material mainly by its calcite, silicate and gypsum (sulphate) content. Combined with mineralogy and screening results, Aura has defined three domains: Domain 1 – calcium-rich. Low aluminium and sulphur; quartz, feldspar, and calcite; represents 16.1% of the mine plan. Forms the surface layer. Domain 2 – sulphur-rich. High sulphur, calcium and strontium from gypsum; represents 23.2% of the mine plan. Sits beneath Domain 1 where present and varies in thickness. Domain 3 – aluminium-rich. High aluminium, phosphorus and potassium, low sulphur and calcium; mainly quartz and feldspar; represents 60.7% of the mine plan. Forms the base of the sequence and the majority of material mined in the first years of operation. Uranium grade shows no correlation with the domains, indicating that all three are similarly mineralised. Mining Plan Mining method Tiris will be mined by conventional, owner-operated open-cut methods. The ore is shallow, flat-lying and free-digging, so no drilling or blasting is needed. Most mineralisation lies within 6 to 8 m of surface. Excavators and wheel loaders will load road-style tipper trucks, which haul ore a short distance to the nearest beneficiation plant. No permanent waste dumps or tailings dams are needed. Waste, coarse beneficiation rejects, and dry-filtered tailings are all returned to mined-out pits. Tailings are placed at the base of the pits and covered with rejects and waste. Clean sand is also mined to support post-leach dewatering (see “Processing”). The mine plan uses two relocatable beneficiation plants, moved seven times over the mine life as mining moves between deposits. Hippolyte East and West ore is hauled to the Hippolyte North plant. Pit optimisation and design Pit optimisations used costs from the BFS Financial Model, recoveries by geometallurgical group, a uranium price of US$90/lb and a 3.5% royalty. Calculated cut-off grades were 151 ppm for Group 1, 100 ppm for Group 2 and 80 ppm for Group 3. The selected cut-offs were 150 ppm for Group 1 and 100 ppm for Groups 2 and 3. The Mineral Resource models use 50 m × 50 m × 1 m blocks. Grade control drilling on a 10 m × 10 m pattern will define a smallest mining unit of about 200 t. Blocks with less than 4% mineralisation were excluded to allow for ore loss, which reduces the mineralised tonnage by about 1–3%. Two sets of Whittle optimisations were run. The first included Measured, Indicated and Inferred material and guided the mine plan. The second used Measured and Indicated material to test the Ore Reserve. Metallurgy Metallurgical test work on Tiris ore has run continuously since 2011, across 27 test programmes. Laboratories and vendors include ANSTO Minerals, RCS/Acclarium, Clean TeQ Water, Vietti Slurrytec, ENPROTEC and Jord International. The test work established the flowsheet and the design parameters summarised in Appendix 4: Material assumptions . Recovery basis The BFS adopts an overall recovery from ore mined to final product of 83.7% as an average over the life of mine. Beneficiation recovers 91.9% of the uranium, and the hydrometallurgical plant recovers 91.1% of the uranium in the leach feed average of the LOM, or 91.5% at steady state. Mineralogy Uranium is hosted solely in carnotite, with grains finer than 5 μm that are well liberated by scrubbing. The gangue is dominated by carbonate minerals (calcite and strontianite), with sulphate minerals (gypsum and celestine) and clays. Because carbonate minerals dominate, alkaline leaching is preferred to acid leaching, which would consume large amounts of acid. Beneficiation The breakthrough for Tiris was finding that screening concentrates most of the carnotite into a small fine fraction. Screening the washed ore recovers more than 91% of the uranium into 12% of the ore mass (life of mine average) at a cut size of 75 µm. Sulphate minerals, which consume leach reagent, tend to report to the coarse fraction and are rejected. In the plant, trucks tip ore over a 200 mm grizzly into a feed hopper. No feeder-breaker is needed because the ore is loose. A rotating drum scrubber washes the carnotite off the coarser particles in about one minute. A hydrocyclone recovers water from the fine-screen oversize. A thickener at each beneficiation plant recovers more water before the slurry is pumped to the Processing Plant. Each beneficiation plant (2.5Mtpa design capacity) is built as modules on skids or trailers, so it can be moved. The mine schedule allows 30 days of downtime for each move. Leaching Alkaline leaching with sodium carbonate and bicarbonate at 80°C extracted 96% of the uranium from the Lazare composites.
